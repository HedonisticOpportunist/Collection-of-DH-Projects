# Digitisation and Historical Studies

This essay explores the digitisation of historical studies, starting with a brief look at its origins. In simple terms, digital tools in historical research have made it easier to access, analyse, and share sources, especially for topics that are scattered, marginalised, or difficult to reach. According to Birnbaum and Kestemont (2017), the advent of digital medieval studies is often attributed to Roberto Busa (1913–2011), a Jesuit theologian who specialised in the lexical analysis of Thomas Aquinas’s works and used a computerised system to manage the scope of the required search. The result of his work was the *Index Thomisticus* project by IBM and Busa, the world’s first sizable machine-readable corpus (p. 1). Nowadays, Nygren and Buckland argue that digitisation has necessitated historians embracing technology in their projects (2014, p. 1). Meanwhile, Salmi states that the digital humanities combine various tools used in the humanities and emphasise using information technology to deepen research. He also notes that Digital History began to emerge in the 1960s, although scholars have paid little attention to it. Lastly, Salmi argues that Digital History is more focused than its umbrella term (Digital Humanities), as it addresses historical problems. It uses tools to examine and represent the past and employs computational methods to analyse and disseminate historical knowledge (2021, pp. 6–7).

Cohen and Rosenzweig (2006) mention that, in the early 1990s, it was predicted that technology would completely replace reliance on the classroom, with books becoming quickly available over networks. However, others were less optimistic, believing that technology would disrupt the tradition of seeking wisdom and depth. In reality, the digital revolution has not led to the classroom's demise. However, daily habits have changed thanks to advances in technology, and history itself has undergone transformation. Historians now use digital tools for research and day-to-day work. Most notably, historians have contributed to the online presence of historical records. In contrast to earlier projects, Nygren and Buckland affirm that recent digital history projects have become more collaborative, inviting users to engage in various ways. Yet the work remains text-focused, and many digital history projects suffer from simplistic interfaces that do not connect to databases to their full potential, limiting the effectiveness of these tools (2014, p. 15).

Additionally, Cohen and Rosenzweig (2006) identify capacity as one of the main advantages of digital media, allowing historians to process large amounts of data and increasing accessibility. This has paved the way for non-academics to explore historical materials, encouraging greater diversity of engagement. Flexibility is another benefit that enables historians to work with these materials in various ways, including manipulating and interacting with data. Lastly, digital media is non-linear, making it possible to weave narratives in multiple ways. Similarly, Nygren and Buckland argue that the benefits of digitisation are obvious, particularly accessibility through open-source materials, which allows people from many different backgrounds to engage with historical sources (2014, p. 16). In another vein, Cohen and Rosenzweig (2006) argue that concerns about quality and durability arise because information can be falsified and can disappear as quickly as it appears. They also argue that readability may suffer because hypertext can make information difficult to locate. Most importantly, they contend that people without access to computers are excluded from technology’s benefits, widening the gap between rich and poor.

Sara Ellis-Nilsson et al. (2026) state that digitising materials is not new within Digital History, with Roberto Busa and his colleagues previously using digitisation to preserve often inaccessible medieval texts. Databases offer a new way of viewing materials and allow comparisons between external artefacts and historical texts (p. 2). Moreover, as Milligan (2022) points out, technological advances have significantly sped up historians’ work compared with the 2000s. However, Milligan also states that not all projects have been digitised, with well-known projects in wealthier countries often favoured over those in developing regions. This means that lesser-known projects are less likely to appear in Google searches, receive citations, and gain scholarly visibility. Furthermore, public-domain works are far more accessible than copyrighted materials and consequently receive more citations.

Milligan (2022) also affirms that digitisation has empowered students and scholars to work on projects that were previously out of reach because barriers such as cost and distance have been eliminated. However, quick access also brings the risk of engaging with materials without sufficient contextual understanding. Similarly, biases remain present in digitisation, as scholars must consider why certain projects have been digitised while others have not. Meanwhile, Kimmo Elo (2020) worries that too much attention has been devoted to creating digital research materials without sufficient consideration of research data, particularly metadata, namely information about content linked to that metadata (p. 104). The creation of metadata is complicated by several factors: determining what should be recorded as metadata, deciding on keywords that make content searchable and coherent, and identifying who is responsible for maintaining it (p. 105). As metadata is often maintained either by private companies or through crowdsourcing (p. 106), materials may become easily searchable but poorly curated, resulting in incomplete records that hinder discovery for research purposes (pp. 106–108).

One example of a digitisation project is the Cantus Database, which “includes descriptions and curated, searchable inventories of manuscript and printed sources from the ninth to the twentieth centuries currently held in libraries, archives, and private ownership all over the world that contain the chant sung during the Middle Ages in Europe” (Lacoste, 2020). It is described as an ongoing project that has existed since the 1980s and is one of the longest-running digital resources in musicology (Lacoste, 2020). Graham et al. (2019) mention that forms of digitisation such as augmented reality (AR) can help users navigate the differences between the present and the past, with these differences experienced not only visually but also through sound. In fact, Graham et al. argue that hearing history can be more impactful than seeing it because fewer interruptions disrupt the user’s experience (pp. 224–225).

Furthermore, within the field of postcolonial narratives, Risam (2019) argues that digital archives, due to their openness, collaborative nature, and accessibility, have enabled a more democratic approach to engaging with historical materials. However, Risam also cautions that this view may be premature, as traces of colonial violence linger within these archives, risking the reinforcement of colonial discourse. For example, former colonial powers such as the British government still carry out digital archival projects, raising concerns about their neutrality and integrity (pp. 47–48). For Risam, one of the opportunities provided by digitisation is the possibility of challenging existing norms through the creation of new tools used to build archives (p. 58). Another factor complicating digitisation, according to Cohen and Rosenzweig (2006), is the loss of information that occurs when analogue artefacts are converted into digital form. Furthermore, scholars often underestimate the costs involved in digitising a project for the first time.

On a personal level, digitisation has significantly accelerated research because it is no longer necessary to make numerous trips to the library, and materials that would otherwise be difficult to locate can be accessed from home. As someone deeply interested in the internment of Japanese Canadians during the Second World War, this has enabled access to articles and scholars who would otherwise have remained unknown, opening new possibilities for future research. One example is the *Database of Names*, created by Michael Abe and a research team at the University of Victoria, which provides “a searchable list of names of 22,000 Japanese Canadian men, women, and children based on pre-war places of origin” (Japanese Canadian Legacies, n.d.).

Anderson (2018) argues that the ability to search for items digitally is not merely a new communicative practice but has also transformed how people carry out daily activities and conceptualise the world. Search engines, for example, have created a cultural expectation that information should be easily discoverable and accessible (pp. 1136, 1142). Bowker et al. (2009) similarly argue that emerging digital technologies, particularly large-scale infrastructures, have forced society to reconsider how information is organised and understood. Such rethinking is necessary to ensure that data can be effectively shared and preserved over long periods of time (pp. 98–99).

As an example of this process, the *Database of Names* is highly structured and metadata-driven, as each entry contains fields such as name, age, pre-war location, and internment site, for example, *Okihiro, Norman Ryukichi (born after uprooting)*. A database organised according to these characteristics allows users to search for individuals using a variety of criteria. However, while such a database enables scholars to learn the names of displaced individuals, searching and filtering records can also distance scholars from the human reality of that suffering, reducing people to data points. Driscoll (2012) argues that “database technology is imagined to serve a dehumanizing function, transforming the individual subject into a bloodless set of statistics” (p. 1). Unlike modern databases that frequently rely on data collected by large corporations such as Facebook (p. 2), the *Database of Names* has been carefully curated and compiled. Moreover, it appears to follow Milligan’s (2013) advice on using such tools responsibly (p. 543), as historians have often fallen into the trap of accepting online databases uncritically because their interfaces and structures appear orderly and authoritative (p. 542).
